A small kitchen rarely has a space problem. It has a layout problem. Most of the room is there, just in the wrong shape: tall cabinets with one shelf, deep corners you can't reach, doors with nothing on them, and a counter that becomes the default storage spot for everything.

The ideas below all work the same way. They take space you already have and make it usable. None of them need a renovation, and most of them work in a rental.

## 1. Go vertical with shelf risers

Kitchen cabinets are usually built with one or two fixed shelves and a lot of empty air above each stack of plates or mugs. A shelf riser splits that height in two, so you can keep small plates underneath and bowls on top without building a tower you have to unstack every morning.

Measure the inside of your cabinet before you buy. You want a riser that is about an inch narrower than the shelf, and short enough that the top layer still clears the next shelf with room for your hand.

## 2. Use the inside of cabinet doors

The back of a cabinet door is one of the most ignored surfaces in a kitchen. It is perfect for flat, light things you reach for often: foil and plastic wrap, cutting boards, pot lids, measuring spoons or cleaning cloths under the sink.

Before you mount anything, close the door slowly and check what it hits. A rack that bumps the shelf edge will keep the door from closing, so leave an inch or two of clearance.

## 3. Decant dry goods into clear containers

Pasta, rice, oats and flour come in bags and boxes that are all different shapes. Moving them into matching clear containers does three things at once: they stack neatly, you can see at a glance what's running low, and pantry moths have a much harder time getting in.

You don't need to decant everything. Start with the five or six things you buy most often. Square or rectangular containers use shelf space better than round ones, and a simple label with the name keeps everyone in the house putting things back in the right place.

## 4. Add a lazy Susan to deep corners

Corner cabinets and the back of deep shelves are where jars go to be forgotten. A turntable brings the back row to the front with one spin. It works in corner cabinets, on pantry shelves, in the fridge and on the counter next to the stove for oils and vinegar.

For corners, pick the largest turntable that still turns freely once the door is open. A raised lip around the edge stops bottles from sliding off when you spin it.

## 5. Hang a rail for utensils and mugs

If your drawers are full, move up. A rail mounted under a wall cabinet or across the backsplash, with a few S-hooks, holds ladles, whisks, mugs or a small herb pot. It clears drawer space and keeps the tools you use most within arm's reach of the stove.

Renting? Look for a rail with adhesive mounts rated for the weight you plan to hang, or use a tension rod inside the window frame.

## 6. Store pot lids upright in a rack

Lids are awkward because they don't stack. Standing them on their edge in a rack, like files in a folder, means you can pull out the one you need without moving the other five. A rack can sit inside a lower cabinet, in a deep drawer or on the back of a cabinet door.

## 7. Keep counters for daily items only

The quickest way to make a small kitchen feel bigger is to clear the counter. A good rule: if you don't use it every day, it doesn't live on the counter. The coffee maker and a utensil crock can stay. The stand mixer, the bread maker and the pile of mail can go somewhere else.

Group the things that do stay into zones. A tray by the stove for oil, salt and pepper. A coffee corner with the machine, mugs and beans. When everything has a place, it's much easier to put it back.

## Where to start

Don't try to do all seven in one weekend. Pick the cabinet or shelf that annoys you most, empty it completely, and set it up with one of the ideas above. Once one zone works, the next one takes half the time.
